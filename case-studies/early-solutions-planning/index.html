Early solution planning retrospective

I Was Practicing Solutions Engineering Before I Knew It Had a Name.

A look back at two of the first internal project plans I created as the sole technical resource at a small property restoration company — before I knew Solutions Engineering was a formal career path.

Privacy note
This case study uses selected excerpts from internal planning documents created in 2023. Company-identifying information and sensitive internal details have been omitted or redacted to preserve the company’s anonymity. The original wording of displayed excerpts is preserved except where [redacted] is shown.
01Context

The assignment was broad. The process was mine to invent.

I joined a small property restoration company as its only internal technical resource. There was no established process for turning a broad technology request into a scoped, approved project, and there was no product or IT team to give me one. If a problem involved software, data, websites, automation, vendors, or another form of technology, it would land with me.

Rather than immediately building whatever was suggested—often involving a new and potentially costly AI tool in the rapidly expanding AI landscape of late 2023—I kept the business goal front and center. I first looked into the problem to determine whether the proposed solution would actually solve it.

That meant I needed a way to show the owners what I was recommending, why I thought it was worth doing, what it might cost, what could go wrong, and how long it could take. I researched common project-plan structures, culled the pieces that were relevant to a small property restoration company, and built a formal proposal structure of my own.

01.2

What I was trying to create & what changed over time

I wanted to give the owners a way to leverage my technical expertise to make informed decisions—not just take my word as gospel. The plans brought together the business problem, proposed approach, MVP, timeline, cost research, risks, contingencies, and expected outcomes. Over time, my process became less focused on how I could implement a request and more deliberate about identifying the problem underneath it, whether something should be built at all, what existing tools or alternatives were available, and how the work could be phased.

02The surviving artifacts

The Surviving Artifacts:Two preserved “Project Plans” show a solutions instinct under refinement.

Neither document is polished. That is the point. They reflect how I was learning to give structure to an instinct I already relied on: turning ambiguous requests into something concrete enough to evaluate, discuss, and base decisions on.

02.1Plan 01 · Website

Untangling an Ambiguous, Broad-Scale Request Spanning Marketing, Development, and Data

The first plan tried to organize several overlapping requests: website performance, lead generation, CRM ambitions, future apps, and system consolidation. One of the better decisions in the document was to narrow the first phase instead of attempting all of it at once.

“The Consolidation phase will focus on resolving the first pain point: Website Optimization.”

The plan then broke that phase into estimated work, documented foreseeable blockers, created a backlog for nonessential features, and listed risks such as scope creep, changing requirements, platform-learning time, late delivery, and technology not meeting expectations.

“Scope creep can mean failure to meet time constraints, additional costs, and even project failure.”
A small but telling writing lesson

This line made me laugh when I found it again. It also illustrates a real point: a decision document should sound decision-ready, not like a stream of internal commentary.

What I now recognize as Solutions Engineering

The Good
  • I was MVP-oriented (Minimum Viable Product). Immediately, I reined in the scope and separated immediate work from future-state ideas.
  • I documented technical uncertainty instead of presenting estimates as guarantees.
Needs Improvement
  • The plan was still too broad: engineering, SEO, content, security, analytics, and marketing work were mixed together.
  • My success criteria were not yet precise enough, and stakeholder ownership was not clearly defined.
  • Anything I put my name on should be as complete and error-free as I can make it. This project reinforced that proofreading is part of technical quality too: incomplete fields, avoidable typos, and stray internal commentary can undermine otherwise sound work.
02.2Plan 02 · Lead generation

Discover. Compare. Scope. Recommend.

The second plan started from a more specific business problem: the company wanted more leads without simply increasing what it was already spending.

Rather than jump straight to building what had been suggested, I tried to define what a minimally useful first version should accomplish and compared third-party tools against the assumption that custom development was automatically the answer. I researched how contractors and home-services companies actually generated leads and quickly discovered how broad the landscape was—advertising, messaging, call tracking, analytics, automation, and custom internal tooling were all in play. From there, I built a feature-and-cost matrix across several messaging and marketing products, including notes on integrations, support, long-term fit, reporting, and automation.

The same document also records an uncomfortable but important realization: the proposed custom build — a web crawler — was much larger than the business problem justified for one person. Part of me wanted to build it because it felt like a way to prove my value as the company’s technical resource and strengthen my credibility. But recognizing that impulse helped me separate what would be impressive to build from what the business actually needed. That realization — and the projects that followed — pushed my planning toward smaller phases, existing tools where appropriate, and more explicit alternatives.

What I now recognize as Solutions Engineering

  • I was thinking in MVPs and incremental delivery even when my first definition of an MVP was still loose.
  • I was learning to separate what would be impressive to build from what the business actually needed.
  • I was identifying risks, but I had not yet developed a disciplined way to justify every probability and impact rating.
Biggest takeaway

The reasoning can be complex. The path for a stakeholder to make a decision should not be.

I had not yet learned how to present the information concisely enough for busy stakeholders to understand the recommendation and make a decision without reading the entire plan.

03Looking back

I did not know the phrase “Solutions Engineering.” I did know I needed a way to make technical decisions legible.

What I took away from this role was how much I enjoyed investigating an unclear problem and delivering something that genuinely helped the business move forward. What I missed was exercising more of my technical depth. Moving forward, Solutions Engineering gives me a name for the balance I’m looking for: solving problems for people while remaining technically hands-on.

04What I would change now

The value of keeping old work is being able to see where your process got better.

I would not use these documents as templates today. I would keep much of the reasoning, but make the decision path shorter, clearer, and easier for stakeholders to act on.

04.1
“A significant improvement” as a success criterion.
Clearly identify and articulate specific evidence that would count as success.
04.2
An uncertain stakeholder section.
  • Identify the decision-maker, users, technical owners, approvers, and people responsible for validating the outcome during discovery.
  • Never present guesswork as settled information in an official document. Verify it, leave it out, or clearly state what remains unknown and how it will be resolved.
04.3
Large plans containing research, implementation detail, risks, and decisions in one long document.
Lead with a short decision-ready recommendation, then keep supporting research and implementation detail behind it.
05Why I am sharing this

These are not polished artifacts. They are evidence of how I learned to create structure when there wasn’t any.

I did not enter this role knowing how to write a solution proposal or how to run technical discovery. I had an ambiguous mandate, real constraints, people waiting for answers, and enough engineering experience to know that jumping straight into implementation would create more problems than it solved.

So I researched, documented, compared, estimated, presented, adjusted, and repeated. The later plans I built for other projects became better because these first ones exposed what I did not yet know. That progression helped me realize there was already a name for the kind of work I had begun gravitating toward: Solutions Engineering.
